# User Guide

## Browse

- Choose a catalog with the Catalog slider. Its stops are ordered by size: Nearest neighbors (22 objects), Nearest 100 objects and Nearest 1000 objects. The label previews a stop while dragging; the catalog switches when the slider is released or changed with the keyboard. The Objects disclosure starts closed; open it to search or use the object buttons, which also work with a keyboard. It lists objects shown on the map by the current distance and type filters; faint background dots remain listed even when they do not pass the magnitude threshold. Search narrows those results further and matches alternate names and catalog designations for every object type, such as HIP 70890, PSR J0633+1746 and M42, as well as molecular-cloud complexes such as Taurus. Spaced and compact identifiers both work (HD 48915 or HD48915). The selected-object card shows Known planets below Object type and Sub-type classifications such as Wolf–Rayet or variable-star classes. Apparent V magnitude follows absolute V magnitude; both use the adopted catalog photometry. Alternate names appear under Designations after the magnitudes, with the row hidden when none are recorded. The Sun has no Constellation row. Published common names, such as Geminga and Lich, appear as the object titles. The heading shows the displayed and total catalog counts, such as `74/1001`. Large result sets use a virtual viewport instead of creating every row at once.
- Catalog switching preserves common object selection, the visibility base, units, filter limits, object types, grid and disclosure settings, and fits the new catalog. Toggling the bright-star overlay or loading/unloading an overlay keeps a camera you moved; from the untouched home view the scene refits to the new visible set. A missing selected object clears selection; a missing visibility base falls back to Sun. Only one scene is active at a time.
- Choose pc or ly for all distance readouts; ly is the default. Colors applies to stars and extended overlays: Real uses restrained temperature and region colors, while Exaggerated (the default) strengthens their separation. Both preferences are remembered on this browser when storage is available. Geometry remains in parsecs and all measurements remain Sun-relative; changing either preference does not move the camera. Source notes are unchanged.
- Preferences sits below Glossary in the control rail. Saved views restore the camera, filters, selection and stellar-motion settings while keeping the current distance units, colors, star-label limit and power saving mode. This also applies to older saved views.
- Object visibility distance is measured from the active origin and defaults to 100 ly. Grid size and cells continue to follow the distance control. The selected object and visibility base remain visible as distance exceptions. Nebulae and molecular clouds use their center distance.
- Below the catalog, five category switches group the map: Compact objects, Stellar systems, Interstellar medium, Stellar remnants and Large-scale structures. The Object type dropdown lists each category's subtypes; a subtype shows only when its category is on and its checkbox is checked, and subtype choices are kept while their category is off. Types without bundled data yet (binaries, multiple systems, dark nebulae, supernova remnants, pulsar-wind nebulae, superbubbles and dust sheets) are disabled with a "No data yet" hint; categories with no data at all are disabled the same way. The Local Bubble is the first object controlled by Bubbles. Sub-brown dwarfs are included in Brown dwarfs, and the Sun keeps its own checkbox under Compact objects. The dropdown summary counts shown types against types with data, such as `4 of 12`.
- Pulsars, Neutron stars and Black holes are opt-in Compact objects subtypes and start unchecked. Enabling any of them loads one frozen compact-remnant overlay; the individual type checkboxes still decide which rows and map objects are shown. Pulsars are the ATNF v2.8.1 source-defined subset inside 3000 ly. The non-pulsar rows are reviewed dormant systems, with Gaia NS1 explicitly shown as a candidate rather than a confirmed neutron star.
- Interstellar medium starts off. Turning it on loads the molecular-cloud and nebula overlays once. Molecular clouds contains all 65 features segmented by Cahlon et al. (2024) from the Leike et al. 3D dust map; their centers span about 378–1435 ly, so use 1500 ly to see the complete source catalog. The nebula overlay contains every catalogued reflection nebula and H II region within about 500 ly (the rho Ophiuchi and Upper Scorpius group, Taurus, Sh 2-27 around zeta Ophiuchi and others) plus the Orion Nebula (about 1265 ly). The planetary nebula Sh 2-216 (about 416 ly) appears under Stellar remnants. Select an extended object from the list or click inside its volume; the focus and zoom controls then fly around it.
- Large-scale structures starts off. Turning it on loads the Local Bubble as a selectable object and draws its translucent dust shell around the Sun. Reset view frames the full reported extent. Most of the cavity remains near its published 170 pc (554 ly) average radius, while the narrower Local Chimney and tunnels reach farther. The displayed grid is downsampled from the source paper's published interactive surface rather than stretched into a uniform 600 pc radius.
- Always show bright stars merges the curated Bright stars catalog into the selected catalog. Shared stable IDs and coincident source positions are deduplicated; the selected catalog keeps its identity and geometry while missing physical fields can be supplied by the bright-star record. Turning it off restores the selected catalog's original membership.
- Earth’s orbit is a 14-stop slider: Off, Now, then January through December. It starts at Now and remembers the selected stop on this browser. Month stops place the marker at noon UTC on the 15th of that month in the current calendar year, giving a representative seasonal position; Now uses the current instant. The Sun-centered ring follows the J2000 ecliptic and has a deliberately exaggerated 0.35 pc display radius so it remains readable from several light-years away—it is not a Solar System distance measurement. The small blue Earth marker moves around the ring while its short pale pole line remains parallel to Earth’s J2000 spin axis, making the changing Sun–axis relationship and 23.44-degree obliquity visible. The selected date remains independent of the stellar-motion timeline.
- Show Milky Way controls the subdued 360-degree sky panorama. It starts enabled, is remembered on this browser, and is loaded only when enabled. The Galactic centre, plane, north pole, and direction of increasing longitude share the map's Galactic-aligned coordinate frame, so the backdrop turns with the same orientation as the star positions and motion arrows.
- Click or tap empty sky to clear selection, its measurement guides, and inspector details without moving the camera. The last selected object stays the visibility base. Orbit drags, pinches, and toolbar actions do not clear selection. Select another dot or catalog entry to restore details and change the base. In Observer view, clicking or tapping another star selects it without moving the camera or showing measurement guides; this latest selection is preserved on exit.
- Selection centers the object by easing the orbit/zoom target over 300 ms while preserving the camera's position. A new selection replaces the transition; direct pointer input, zoom buttons, reset, or deselection interrupt it immediately. Reduced-motion preferences use instant focus and disable the selection pulse; enabling reduced motion during focus completes it immediately. Drag with the primary mouse button or one finger to orbit. Use the wheel or two-finger pinch to zoom; right-drag or a two-finger drag pans.
- The toolbar provides reset-view, grid, zoom, selection-history, Set as origin, and Observer view buttons. Set as origin moves the grid and measurement origin to the selected object and recalculates object-list distances. Observer view places the camera at the object selected on entry, hides that observing object, and allows rotation only; zoom, pan, and reset are disabled until the mode is exited. Its compact Observing from card identifies the observing object; click its name to select that object again. The same card rolls the camera clockwise or counterclockwise in 15-degree steps and resets the roll to level. The roll stays fixed while dragging, drags follow the rolled screen, and the view stops just short of straight up or down. Other objects can be selected from the scene or Objects list without moving the camera or drawing distance guides. Exiting preserves the latest selection and applies the normal Reset-view framing. The grid toggle hides the grid, all three axis lines and their labels, and the grid legend, leaving stars, measurements, and the camera unchanged.
- The inspector shows a conservative plain-language object type, Earth-view constellation and Sun distance. Stellar rows show spectral class, temperature, bolometric luminosity, mass, radius, metallicity, age and absolute Johnson V magnitude. Pulsars instead show rotation (period and frequency), 1400 MHz radio luminosity, characteristic age, surface magnetic field and spin-down power when ATNF supplies them; pulsar mass remains `Not available` because companion-mass limits are not pulsar masses. Other neutron-star and black-hole systems show mass, confirmation status, orbital period, visible companion and detection method. Molecular clouds show their named complex, catalog mass, equivalent radius, mean and peak density, segmented volume, source-voxel count and primary reference. Nebulae show designations, apparent size, modeled extent, illuminating stars, distance source and a primary reference. The Local Bubble shows its designations, average radius, shell-distance range, typical thickness and source. Coordinates & source contains epoch, motion data and source notes.
- On narrow screens, the inspector scrolls below the scene, without covering it. Compact heading spacing leaves more sky visible; toolbar hover tooltips appear only for fine, hover-capable pointers, while keyboard-focus tooltips and 44 px targets remain available on all devices.

## Visibility

Stellar visibility uses apparent Johnson V magnitude from the selected or last-selected object's position: `m_V = M_V + 5 log10(distance_pc / 10)`. The Filter slider defaults to +7, approximately one magnitude beyond dark-sky naked-eye +6, and ranges from 0 to 25. The unrounded inclusive threshold decides eligibility, not halo size/intensity. Higher limits include fainter stellar objects. Enabled compact remnants are locator symbols rather than visible-light photometry, so the V-magnitude slider does not suppress them. Nebulae and molecular clouds are extended objects without a point magnitude; the slider does not hide them or their names. Only the loaded catalog is considered, not the complete sky from that location; extinction and variability are not modeled.

The visibility base and eligible objects retain approximately 10 CSS px crisp cores. Other objects are 3 CSS px temperature-colored dots with no halo, name or motion arrow, but unchanged generous mouse/touch hit targets. Missing V photometry and distinct objects at the same stored position are background, never assumed bright. Selecting any object makes it the base and keeps it prominent even without photometry; deselection removes only its selection boost, ring and guides. Approximate co-located binary coordinates are not a physical zero separation or a license to invent component brightness.

## Star Appearance

Compact-remnant locators use fixed illustrative colors: cyan for pulsars, violet for other neutron stars and orange for black holes. Their neutral glow is a locator, not a claim about optical brightness, event horizons or physical diameter.

Nebulae are drawn as soft, overlapping glows filling an approximate 3D volume at physical scale. The Orion Nebula is a bowl open toward the Sun, brightest on its back wall behind the Trapezium; the Pleiades dust lies in two sheets along the line of sight; Sh 2-216 is a hollow shell; other nebulae are clumpy ellipsoids. Colors run from the illuminated core outward: Real uses muted pink/white emission and pale blue reflection light, Exaggerated stronger teal, magenta and red emission and deeper blue. Volumes, depth and orientation are illustrative models constrained by angular size and published geometry, not density maps, and brightness is not calibrated surface brightness.

Molecular clouds follow the published one-parsec 3D voxel geometry. One deterministically distributed quarter of the 78,326 source voxels is rendered as overlapping, noise-shaped puffs: a subdued smoky body preserves opacity and a restrained luminous rim picks out filaments. The dust-like palette stays within charcoal, taupe and cool gray; Real is darker and Exaggerated raises contrast without assigning rainbow colors to separate clouds. Puff radius, color and brightness are illustrative display styling rather than measured cloud edges, temperature or surface brightness. The catalog positions, shapes, masses, equivalent radii and density values remain source data.

The Local Bubble uses one compact indexed shell, pale blue additive blending and angle-weighted opacity. It remains faint but readable from inside and becomes stronger along grazing parts of the irregular wall. The layer adds one draw call, writes no depth, performs no ray marching and does no per-frame CPU shape work. Its shell is a downsampled peak-extinction surface rather than a density volume.

Eligible halos retain a diameter of `clamp(68 - 4.25 * absolute_mag, 20, 80)` CSS px: Sirius A roughly 62 px and Barnard's Star 20 px. A base without magnitude uses 30 px. Sizes do not vary with camera zoom. Cores use camera depth for overlap; halos blend additively without writing depth or bleaching core centers. Hot and solar-temperature stars use a hot-white center; below 5000 K that white contribution tapers so red dwarfs retain more red, while brown and sub-brown dwarfs receive only a small white lift. The shared halo texture combines a soft radial bloom with restrained four-axis rays, so brighter objects shine more strongly without adding a mesh or draw call per star. The Real illustrative temperature ramp runs from cold red to blue-white, clamped to 250-40,000 K; Exaggerated uses the same temperatures with stronger warm and cool separation. A star with no measured temperature uses only its O/B/A/F/G/K/M spectral class for display color; an unknown class and a temperature-less white dwarf remain neutral gray. Brown and sub-brown dwarfs instead use a brown ramp from darker Y-dwarf browns (250 K) to lighter tan (2400 K), every shade at least 4.5:1 against the black sky. A blank brown dwarf temperature falls back to its spectral class only for this shade (M 2400 K, L 1800 K, T 1000 K, Y 350 K, otherwise 1300 K). Display-color stand-ins are never shown or stored as temperatures. A colored locator is not a claim of optical brightness. These are neither calibrated spectra nor stellar radii.

For eligible objects, intrinsic Johnson V absolute magnitude controls halo strength: `clamp(0.65 * 10^(-0.1 * (absolute_mag - 4.83)), 0.08, 1.4)`, with a clamped exponent for extreme inputs. A missing-magnitude base uses neutral gain 0.65; the Sun now has the sourced absolute V value +4.83. Bolometric luminosity is never substituted. Selection boosts gain by 1.3, capped at 1.8. Opacity is `0.9 * (1 - exp(-1.4 * gain))`, preserving temperature tint without whitening. The texture fades continuously without an annular hole; strict depth testing keeps it off its own core without a dark outline. The glow remains an intrinsic visual-brightness cue, not apparent-brightness scaling.

## Interface And Labels

The interface uses night-light orange text and accents on charcoal panels, a Helvetica Neue-style native system font stack, and temperature-based object colors. The optional Milky Way panorama remains deliberately faint so foreground data stays legible. Map labels show only the object name, without a spectral-class subtitle, at a fixed 22 CSS pixel offset to the right of the dot during orbit and zoom. Unselected names hide at collisions or edges instead of switching sides. Their stacking and collision priority follow camera depth, nearest first, rather than catalog order; farther dots and arrows do not hide nearer names. Interface controls, nearer objects, and a star's own arrow still reserve space. The selected name uses heavier, lightened text and a temperature-colored underline, with a matching glowing ring and inspector identity accent. Its brief ring pulse does not loop. The selected name always stays in the foreground with a dark backing, ignoring collisions; it clamps to the scene edges when necessary, even when the star is offscreen or behind the camera. Its selection ring and motion arrow still disappear when the actual star is clipped. Spectral classes remain available in the inspector.

## Supporting Graphics

Supporting graphics stay secondary: unselected motion arrows use 50% opacity and the selected arrow uses full opacity, without changing any dimensions or visibility rules. The grid fades from 46% opacity near the center to transparent at its outer radius, with display-DPI compensation up to 90% so its one-pixel lines remain legible on Retina-class screens; axes use 55% opacity. The blue Earth-orbit ring is fixed in the J2000 ecliptic plane while its date marker and spin-axis cue are screen-readable reference marks, not scale claims. Axis captions retain a fixed 18 CSS pixel right offset from their projected endpoints and render behind the transparent canvas, so stars cover them. They never dodge stars or star names; only viewport and interface clipping can hide them. The sky panorama is sampled directly from one optimized 2560×1280 texture with no cube-map expansion or mipmap allocation; disabling it restores the black background. Selected measurement guides retain their original emphasis. These effects do not fade stars or their labels.

## Motion Arrows

Small arrows on eligible objects indicate projected motion. The Arrow length filter selects 1k, 5k, 10k, 25k, or 50k years; 1k years is the default. Each center-to-tip length represents the displayed velocity's linear travel over that interval at the map's parsec scale, so it changes with zoom and foreshortens as motion points toward or away from the camera. The shaft starts at the edge of the star dot without adding that gap to the represented distance. Solid arrows use complete three-dimensional motion in the existing approximate Galactic-rest frame, including the Sun. Dashed shafts show only the known Sun-relative transverse displacement derived from parallax and proper motion when radial velocity is unavailable or withheld. Arrowheads and strokes retain the original 16 CSS px styling. Arrows are drawn in the canvas above stars and halos but beneath map names; dashed shafts use 2 CSS px dashes and 1.33 CSS px gaps while arrowheads stay solid. Arrows do not intercept clicks, and positions remain static rather than being propagated.

The velocity frame and linear projection are described in [Motion Arrows](data-reference.md#motion-arrows).
