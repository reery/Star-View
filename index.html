      Derived from catalog values, which may be measured or model-derived. Gravity = GM/r²; escape speed = √(2GM/r); mean density = 3M/(4πr³). These spherical Newtonian estimates omit rotation, radiation pressure, and relativity. Extra digits do not imply extra accuracy.

      About temperature & the spectrum

      Effective temperature describes the temperature of a blackbody emitting the same total power per unit surface area as the object. It is not the core temperature. Catalog values may be measured, estimated or model-derived; missing individual temperatures are not inferred.

      The curves use Planck’s law for emission per unit wavelength, each scaled to its own peak of 1 to compare spectral shape rather than brightness. The dashed curve represents the origin when it has a known individual temperature and differs from the selected object; otherwise it represents the Sun, when distinct. Wien’s law gives λpeak = 2,897,772 nm K / T. This is a continuum estimate, not an observed spectrum; absorption lines, atmospheres, dust and companion light are omitted. The wavelength axis is logarithmic. A peak in visible light does not mean the star looks that single color: its appearance combines many wavelengths.

      The temperature bar is logarithmic, with approximate M–O temperature guides for main-sequence stars. O stars extend beyond 30,000 K. Giants, white dwarfs and brown dwarfs have different spectral calibrations. Out-of-range temperatures are marked at the nearest endpoint with an arrow. Marker colors follow the scene setting; the perceived-color glow uses the app’s approximate real colors, brightened for visibility. The Sun’s nominal effective temperature is 5,772 K.

      Temperature ratios use kelvin: 2× means twice the absolute temperature.

      Calculations & sources

      The result is the adopted catalog bolometric luminosity, matching the object specifications, comparisons and scales. It may be measured, estimated or model-derived; it is not an exact known value. Surface area A = 4πR² uses the catalog radius, assuming a spherical photosphere. Mean surface flux F = L/A is derived from that luminosity and area, not measured independently. The factors are shown relative to solar surface area A☉ = 4πR☉² and solar surface flux F☉ = L☉/A☉, so (A/A☉) × (F/F☉) = L/L☉. This illustrates how the catalog luminosity is distributed across the surface; it does not independently determine luminosity. A separately sourced temperature is not used to recompute L. Missing radius leaves both factors unavailable while retaining the catalog luminosity.

      Incident flux F = L/(4πd²). To match the origin’s flux at 1 AU, d = √(L/Lorigin) AU. When the origin’s flux is unavailable, the Sun’s flux at 1 AU is used instead. Distances share a linear scale from the star’s center. Flux is measured above an atmosphere on a surface facing the star, not averaged over Earth. Equal bolometric flux does not imply the same spectrum, climate or habitability. These comparisons omit extinction, variability and companion illumination; distances inside a known photosphere are unavailable. Orbit comparisons use the closest Solar System planet’s semi-major axis, Mercury through Neptune, only within 20%; these are orbital sizes, not current planet positions.

      Both luminosity scales are logarithmic. The broad scale spans 10⁻⁶–10⁶ L☉ and expands for outliers; these endpoints are display limits. Marker color follows the app’s stellar color setting, not luminosity.

      1 L☉ = 3.828 × 10²⁶ W; R☉ = 695,700 km; 1 AU = 149,597,870,700 m. The nominal solar luminosity gives approximately 1,361 W/m² at 1 AU. Displayed values use three significant digits.

      About the scales & sources

      Lower magnitudes mean brighter light. A difference of 5 magnitudes means 100 times the visible brightness. The equal-distance visible brightness ratio is 100.4(MSun − Mselected), using the adopted absolute V magnitudes. V measures visible light in the Johnson V band, rather than total bolometric luminosity.

      Each bar uses equal steps in magnitude, with fainter objects on the left and brighter ones on the right. The apparent bar focuses on nighttime objects, normally +16 to −6, and expands for outliers or the selected visibility guide. The Sun is omitted from that bar.

      Absolute magnitude compares stars at the same distance. Apparent values remain Earth-view catalog snapshots when the origin changes; they do not predict current variable-star brightness. Missing individual measurements are left unavailable. Unresolved system photometry is not plotted as an individual component.

      Venus is shown near its brightest (−4.9); Sirius at −1.46. The icon buttons select approximate stellar visibility limits: naked eye +6, typical 40–50 mm binoculars +10, a 100–150 mm amateur telescope +13, or a large professional observatory near +27 with long-exposure imaging. These are representative guides, not universal averages. Each band fades over the two magnitudes approaching its limit; actual detection depends on sky brightness, equipment, exposure, eyesight and an object's extent. Observatory imaging is a camera detection limit, rather than an eyepiece view. Marker colors follow the app's stellar color setting.

      About this diagram

      Temperature decreases to the right on a logarithmic scale. Lower magnitudes are brighter. The spectral letters are approximate temperature guides for dwarfs; giants and white dwarfs have different spectral calibrations.

      Visual uses the catalog’s Johnson V magnitude. Bolometric uses total luminosity: Mbol = 4.74 − 2.5 log₁₀(L/L☉). Missing values are not inferred. Regions are schematic guides, not classification boundaries; L, T and Y extend into cool substellar objects.

      The blue-to-red background is a temperature guide; the coolest objects emit mainly infrared light. Positions use adopted catalog values, which may be measured, model-derived or estimated.

      About this diagram

      M-K (Morgan–Keenan) places the catalog’s spectral subtype against its luminosity class. The classes describe spectral features associated with stellar surface gravity; they are not equal steps in absolute magnitude.

      Only explicit O–M subtypes and luminosity classes are plotted. Intermediate classes are shown between rows. Missing classes are not inferred from temperature or brightness. White dwarfs and L, T and Y dwarfs use separate classification schemes.

      The white-dwarf D classification uses spectral features rather than an M-K luminosity class. Additional letters are retained in the catalog label; a number, as in DA2, is a temperature index.

      Units, imagery & assumptions

      The radius bars use logarithmic scales. The reference bar is centered on 1× the chosen object and expands by powers of ten to include the selected radius. The typical-size bar marks an approximate range for the object’s class; its guide tick is the geometric middle of that range, not a measured population average. The logarithmic axis extends to include values outside the range, with a thinner line beyond the marked boundaries.

      Based on catalog radii, which may be measured, estimated, or model-derived. Area and volume are derived, not independent measurements.

      Stars are represented as spheres using their catalog radii. Diameter = 2 × radius; surface area = 4πr²; volume = 4πr³/3. These derived values inherit the catalog radius’s uncertainty; extra digits do not imply extra accuracy. Stellar surface detail, shine, and temperature-based colors are illustrative. Cool dwarfs are brightened for visibility, although much of their radiation is infrared.

      R☉ = 695,700 km. R♃ = 71,492 km and R⊕ = 6,378.1 km (equatorial radii). Jupiter and Earth are oblate spheroids with polar radii 66,854 km and 6,356.8 km; their area and volume account for this flattening.

      Jupiter uses a Hubble photographic map with the Great Red Spot facing the viewer; the unobserved small polar caps extend the map’s nearest observed row. Earth uses NASA’s Blue Marble satellite mosaic with its historical cloud composite. Planetary shading adds illustrative daylight and grazing-angle atmospheric haze. These views show recognizable features, not the current rotation or illumination.
